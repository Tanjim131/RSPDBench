RSPDBench: Benchmarking Vision Foundation Models on Earth Observation Tasks Under Physically Grounded Remote-Sensing Product Degradations

Tanjim Bin FarukKhondaker Masfiq RezaShrideep PallickaraSangmi Lee Pallickara

Paper (arXiv) Code BibTeX

RSPDBench measures how much Earth-observation foundation models lose when the satellite image they read has lost resolution, has a calibration error, has misaligned bands or is noisy.

What goes wrong before a model sees the image

A satellite image reaches a model only after a long chain of processing. The optics blur it, the detectors add noise, calibration sets its brightness, and the bands, recorded at different resolutions and at different places on the focal plane, are resampled onto one grid and aligned with each other. Each step is accurate only to a tolerance, so a delivered product can be blurrier, brighter or darker, or noisier than intended, or have bands slightly out of line.

Benchmarks for Earth-observation foundation models score them on clean images, and distribution-shift benchmarks change place, season and sensor together, so a failure cannot be traced to one cause. RSPDBench keeps each scene fixed and changes one defect at a time, plus two chains that combine them, at three strengths taken from published Sentinel-2 and Landsat 8 specifications. Each defect is applied to the product in its own units, before any model-specific preprocessing, and the benchmark measures how far each model's score drops from its own clean score.

Six defects, each set by a published sensor specification

Four primitives isolate one failure each; two chains combine them the way real acquisitions and harmonized products do. Severities S1 to S3 follow the Sentinel-2 and Landsat 8 specifications. Select a card to see that defect in the explorer.

P1

Resolution loss

Optical blur and a coarser detector: a Gaussian blur, averaging over \(N \times N\) pixels, then resizing back to the original grid.

Levels
\(N \in \{1, 2, 4\}\), \(\sigma = N/2\)
Anchor
keeps \(19\text{–}29\%\) of the contrast at the coarse Nyquist frequency; Sentinel-2 specifies \(15\text{–}30\%\)
P2

Calibration gain

Every band multiplied by the same factor, as an absolute-calibration or cross-sensor error would. Band ratios such as NDVI are unchanged.

Levels
\(p = 1 \pm \alpha\), \(\alpha \in \{3, 8, 15\}\%\)
Anchor
Sentinel-2 requires \(<5\%\) (goal \(3\%\)); Landsat 8 OLI \(\pm 3\%\)
P3

Band misregistration

The red-edge, narrow-NIR and SWIR bands shift together against the fixed visible bands, so a pixel's bands no longer see the same ground.

Levels
\(\Delta \in \{0.3, 1, 2\}\) px
Anchor
Sentinel-2 requires \(<0.3\) of the coarser pixel at \(3\sigma\), i.e. \(0.6\) px at \(10\,\mathrm{m}\)
P4

Sensor noise

Gaussian noise in each band, scaled to that band's mean signal: \(\hat{x}_c = x_c + k\,\mu_c\,z_c\).

Levels
\(\mathrm{SNR} \in \{100, 33, 20\}\), \(k = 1/\mathrm{SNR}\)
Anchor
Sentinel-2 requires \(\mathrm{SNR} = 72\text{–}174\) at reference radiance
C1

Poor acquisition

Gain error, blur and noise in one capture, with the noise added on the coarse grid: \(\hat{x} = U_N\big(D_N(G_\sigma * p\,x) + \epsilon\big)\).

Levels
P2, P1 and P4 at the same severity, both gain directions
C2

Harmonization residuals

Infrared bands resampled and shifted (group \(\mathcal{G}\)) and rescaled (group \(\mathcal{H}\)) when bands or sensors are merged; visible bands stay fixed.

Levels
P1 and P3 on \(\mathcal{G}\), gain on \(\mathcal{H}\), same severity
Effect
band-selective, so ratios such as NDVI change

Paper Sec. 3.4

Checked against the sensor itself

Sentinel-2 measures near-infrared twice: band B08 at 10 m and band B8A at 20 m, over almost the same wavelengths. Passing the real 10 m band through P1's moderate setting reproduces the real 20 m band of the same scene. Across 3,000 test patches, the best-fitting blur width is \(\sigma = 0.75\text{–}1.5\) pixels, bracketing the benchmark's \(\sigma = 1\). The real misregistration between the two bands is only \(0.02\text{–}0.05\) px, so P3's \(1\) and \(2\) px levels are deliberate stress tests.

Main findings

Paper Sec. 4.5

Robustness rankings do not transfer

Rank the foundation models by how much they lose to resolution loss (left) and to a calibration gain error (right), at the severe setting. On four of the five datasets the two orders are close to reversed. RAMEN, which conditions on resolution, loses the least to blur on every dataset and the most to gain error on every satellite dataset; the DINOv3 models are the reverse. Generic corruption benchmarks mostly test blur and noise, so ranked on blur alone RAMEN would be picked as the most robust model, although it is the one most hurt by a calibration error.

When defects compound

The acquisition chain C1 applies a gain error, blur and coarse-grid noise together. Its excess is the drop beyond its worst single component. It peaks when blur has weakened a model without saturating it; at the most severe setting there is little left to lose.

Paper Sec. 4.7

A smarter readout helps, but not with blur

Retraining only the linear readout on degraded training views, with the encoder still frozen, removes most of the noise and harmonization loss on m-BigEarthNet. Resolution loss still costs about a fifth of the clean score, because the detail is gone from the encoder's features. This is a best case: the readout was trained on labeled images carrying the same defect types as the test images. A deployed model rarely has such labels or knows which defects to expect, so it would have to adjust from unlabeled images alone, and whether that can bring back detail lost to blur is an open question.

Models and clean scores

Each foundation model runs under its own author-style frozen protocol with a clean-trained light head; clean scores anchor every relative drop. They are not a cross-model ranking, because heads and input contracts differ.

Code

Run the degradations on your own model

The operators work on any (bands, height, width) tensor in native product units. Apply them before your model's own normalization, keep the head trained on clean data, and report the drop from your clean score.

# pip install rspdbench
import rspdbench as rb

contract = rb.get_contract("hls-burn-scars")
bands = ["BLUE", "GREEN", "RED", "NIR_NARROW", "SWIR_1", "SWIR_2"]
for cond in rb.conditions_for(contract, bands):        # 37 conditions
    y = rb.degrade(x, cond, band_ids=bands, contract=contract,
                   sample_id=tile_id, band_mean=train_band_means)
    # your preprocessing -> frozen encoder -> clean-trained head -> metric
